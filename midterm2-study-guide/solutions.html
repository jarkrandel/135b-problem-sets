    Problem 1: Suppose that a Markov chain has the following transition matrix:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1/2 & 1/2 & 0 & 0\\ 1/3 & 0 & 2/3 & 0 & 0\\ 1/4 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 3/4\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1/2 & 1/2\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Determine the classes and identify whether each is recurrent or transient.
    🔗
    Solution: Drawing the diagram for this chain, we see that the classes are \(\{1,2,3\},\ \{4\},\) and \(\{5\}\text{.}\) The class \(\{1,2,3\}\) is transient since \(3\rightarrow 5\) implies it is not closed. Similarly, \(\{4\}\) is transient since \(4\rightarrow 5\text{.}\) Finally, \(\{5\}\) is absorbing, hence recurrent.
    🔗
    Problem 2: Suppose that a branching process has offspring distribution given by
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} p_0 = \frac{1}{5},\quad p_1 = \frac{1}{2},\quad p_2 = \frac{1}{10},\quad p_3 = \frac{1}{5}, \end{equation*}
    and \(p_k = 0\) for all other positive integers \(k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint: It should be helpful to know that \(2s^3+s^2-5s+2 = (s-1)(2s^2+3s-2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Compute the probability that the bacteria population ever dies out.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Suppose that you know that the population has 30 individuals at generation 22. What is the expected number of individuals at generation 28?
      🔗
      🔗
    Solution:
    🔗
    1. We have
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \phi(s) = \frac{1}{5} + \frac{1}{2}s + \frac{1}{10}s^2 + \frac{1}{5}s^3. \end{equation*}
      So,
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} s = \phi(s) \iff s &= \frac{1}{5} + \frac{1}{2}s + \frac{1}{10}s^2 + \frac{1}{5}s^3. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
      Multiplying through by 10 and rearranging, we get \(\phi(s) = s\) iff
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} 0 = 2s^3 + s^2 - 5s + 2 = (s-1)(2s^2+3s-2) = (s-1)(2s-1)(s+2). \end{equation*}
      The smallest solution to this in \([0,1]\) is \(s = \frac{1}{2}\text{,}\) so \(\pi_0 = \frac{1}{2}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The mean of the offspring distribution is
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \mu = 0\cdot\frac{1}{5} + 1\cdot\frac{1}{2} + 2\cdot\frac{1}{10} + 3\cdot\frac{1}{5} = \frac{5 + 2 + 6}{10} = \frac{13}{10}. \end{equation*}
      Since each of the 30 individuals at generation 22 evolves its own family tree independently of the others, and each tree has \(\mu^6\) expected individuals 6 generations later, the answer is
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} 30\mu^6 = 30\left(\frac{13}{10}\right)^6. \end{equation*}
      🔗
    Problem 3: Label the four vertices of a square \(1,2,3,4\) in clockwise order. Suppose that an ant transitions between vertices at each time step in one of two ways.
    🔗
    • With probability \(\frac{1}{3}\text{:}\) If the ant is at vertices 1 or 2, it does not move. If it is at vertex 3, it transitions to 4. If it is at vertex 4, it transitions to 3.
      🔗
      🔗
    • With probability \(\frac{2}{3}\text{:}\) The ant moves to the next vertex in clockwise order.
      🔗
      🔗
    1. Write down the transition matrix for the Markov chain whose state at step \(n\) is the vertex that the ant occupies at time \(n\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Compute \(\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} P^n_{ij}\text{,}\) for any states \(i\) and \(j\text{.}\) Justify your computation and your usage of any theorems (this doesn’t require a long calculation).
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Compute the long-run proportion of steps on which the ant transitions from an even state to an odd state.
      🔗
      🔗
    Solution:
    🔗
    1. We have transitions \(1\rightarrow 2,\ 2\rightarrow 3,\ 3\rightarrow 4,\ 4\rightarrow 1\) with probability \(\frac{1}{3}\) and transitions \(1\rightarrow 1,\ 2\rightarrow 2,\ 3\rightarrow 4,\ 4\rightarrow 3\) with probability \(\frac{2}{3}\text{.}\) Collecting these into the transition matrix, we get
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} P = \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 & 2/3 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 1/3 & 2/3 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1\\ 2/3 & 0 & 1/3 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
      🔗
    2. This finite chain is irreducible since \(1 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 3 \rightarrow 4 \rightarrow 1\text{.}\) This chain is aperiodic since \(1\rightarrow 1\) (implying state 1 has period 1, so the whole chain has period 1). Therefore, \(\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}P^n_{ij} = \pi_j\) for any \(i,j\) where \(\pi\) is the invariant distribution of the chain. Since this matrix is doubly stochastic, \(\pi_j = 1/4\) for all \(j\text{,}\) and so the limit is \(1/4\) for all \(i,j\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. This is just \(\pi_2(P_{21} + P_{23}) + \pi_4(P_{41} + P_{43}) = \frac{1}{4}(\frac{2}{3} + 0) + \frac{1}{4}(\frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{3}) = \frac{5}{12}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Problem 4: Bob made a New Year’s resolution to exercise more regularly. He randomly chooses to exercise or not exercise each day depending on how many times he exercised within the previous two days:
    🔗
    • If Bob exercised 0 times within the previous two days, then he exercises with probability 1.
      🔗
      🔗
    • If Bob exercised \(i\in\{1,2\}\) times within the previous two days, then he exercises with probability \(1-\frac{i}{3}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Consider the Markov chain with states \(1,2,3,4\) labeled \(NN, NE, EN, EE\) respectively where \(X_n = NN\) if Bob did not exercise on days \(n-1\) and \(n\text{,}\) \(X_n = NE\) if Bob did not exercise on day \(n-1\text{,}\) but did exercise on day \(n\text{,}\) etc. Suppose that Bob did not exercise on the two days before New Year’s Day (labeled days \(-1,0\)) so that \(X_0 = 1 = NN\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Write out the transition matrix \(P\) for this chain using the labeling of states given above.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Write an expression for the probability that Bob exercises on days \(3,4,\) and \(6\text{,}\) but not on day 5.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Write an expression in terms of \(P\) for the expected number of days Bob exercises on days \(8\) and \(9\) combined.
      🔗
      🔗
    Solution:
    🔗
    1. We have
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1/3 & 2/3\\ 1/3 & 2/3 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 2/3 & 1/3 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
    2. We are looking for the probability that days \(3,4,5,6\) are labeled \(EENE\text{.}\) This is the same as saying that \(X_4 = EE\) and \(X_6 = NE\text{.}\) Since \(X_0 = NN\text{,}\) we can write this as
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} P_{14}^4 P_{42}^2. \end{equation*}
      You could also write the second two-step transition probability as a product of one-step probabilities \(P_{43}P_{32}\) since it’s also true that \(X_5=EN\text{.}\) So, the answer is equivalently
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} P_{14}^4P_{43}P_{32}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
    3. The value of \(X_9\) determines how many times Bob exercised on days \(8\) and \(9\text{.}\) Values of \(1,2,3,4\) for \(X_9\) correspond to \(0,1,1,2\) days of exercise respectively. Since \((\alpha P^9)_i = \prob(X_9=i)\text{,}\) the answer is
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \prob(X_9=2) + \prob(X_9=3) + 2\prob(X_9=4) = [1,0,0,0]\cdot P^9\cdot \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
